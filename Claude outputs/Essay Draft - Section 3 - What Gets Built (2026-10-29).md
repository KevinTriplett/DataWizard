## Section 3: what gets built

I am going to describe the system as five parts, and I want to say first how to read them, because the form matters as much as the content. This is not a plan with stages. Every one of the five is valuable on its own, can be entered without the others existing, and has a stated answer to the question "what breaks if this is removed." Theories like this one die when they are written as sequences, because a sequence fails at its first missed step; what survives is a set of parts that can be entered in any order and that strengthen each other when more than one is present. So for each part I will say what it is, what the evidence for it is, what it needs from the others, and what it can do alone. The order below is the order of explanation, not of construction. If I had to say which part could start tomorrow with the least risk, it is the fourth.

### The ground: the substrate the parts stand on

Everything below runs on technology that implements the protocols I described in the last section, and I mean everything, the social parts included, because the interactions I am about to describe take place inside tools built on it. That is a strong claim and the reason for it is simple: without it, each part works at one locality, on whatever platform is to hand, until the platform changes its terms or the group outgrows it or the next administration shuts it down, and then it is gone, the way Porto Alegre's participatory budget was gone when the mayor changed `[cite: Section 4 carries the full case]`. The substrate is what makes a local experiment into something that can survive its host.

What it provides is small and specific. A person or a group has an identity it controls, with no registry in the middle. People have relationships that both sides acknowledge. A group is a first-class thing - it has members, roles and rules of its own - rather than a label on a company's server. Credentials can be issued, held and shown without a platform. And a group's data persists outside any one application, so that an app can die and the group does not. Those five primitives are all that the rest of the system asks of the ground.

Two things get built on it, and I want to be precise about the state of each, because both of you will check. The first is new tools. A group describes the pain it has - the thing it cannot do, or does badly, in what it already does - and a language model generates the interface and the workflow, while the substrate supplies the parts generated software gets wrong: who is who, who belongs, who may do what, and where the data lives. There is one working instance of the generating half, without a substrate under it: a project in the Bay Area that has spent more than a year letting people who are not developers build small experimental tools for their own communities[^19]. Generation against a substrate has not been done. The second is existing software. Nothing is rewritten. An app a group already uses adds one capability - a sign-in that speaks the protocol - beside the sign-in it already has, keeps its database and its users, and adds more capabilities over time: a consequential action becomes a signed document; a membership becomes a credential issued beside the membership row the app already keeps; and at that point, with no visible feature having changed, the group's membership is portable, recognizable by another community's tools without anyone building a bridge between them. Only at the far end of that ladder does the group's record of truth start to leave the app's database, and every step before it is reversible. This has been specified against one real codebase and executed at the scale of none[^20]. Functional pluralism - a church's ancient membership software with one sidecar, a self-hosted forum, a group on a thirty-million-person open network - is the strategy, because "everyone must use the pure form of the substrate" will not meet reality and does not need to.

The honest status, dated, because it will change: as of October 2026 there are three libraries that implement a substrate, all early and moving weekly[^21]; one tool-builder in use by non-developers, without a substrate; and no tool built on a substrate in use by any group. Apart from the tool-builder, this layer stands on theory. I would rather you read that here than discover it.

There is one more thing to say about the ground, which is why it needs to be protocols and not a very good platform, and the answer is a short list of things a platform cannot do however well it is built. It cannot give a group a track record that travels - that another community's tools can read - because the record is the platform's asset, not the group's. It cannot count heads credibly without a central registry, which is the thing being avoided. It cannot let a vulnerable group deliberate without the platform itself being able to enumerate who was in the room. And it cannot keep a public record of what officials committed to and what happened that the platform itself cannot edit. Those four are the irreducible ones. The last deserves a sentence of its own, because the word "blockchain" will come up and I want to place it: the function - a public, append-only, tamper-evident record that no party including us can alter - is non-negotiable, since it is what makes the record believable to the people it is used against. A blockchain is one way to get it, the most expensive, and the one with the worst record of serving the communities it claims to. The same function is delivered by the transparency logs that already secure the web's certificates, by witnessed logs of the kind the identity community now builds, or by a ledger run jointly by the library, the county clerk, the newspaper and the college[^22]. A public chain is justified where no set of plural local witnesses can be trusted, and that is a fact about a community, not a preference about technology.

### Part one: the groups that already exist

The entry is not governance, and it is not a new community either. Both are cold-start problems: a governance process has no one in it on day one, and a community formed from nothing takes years and an organizer. The entry is a group that already exists - a congregation, a co-op, a parents' group at a school, a mutual-aid network, a crew that fixes things, a club - and already carries the two things that make people keep showing up, which are relationships and meaning. The offer to that group is a tool that removes a named pain in what it already does. Per group, this is what Andrew Chen calls an atomic network: valuable at the size the group already is, with no dependence on anyone outside it adopting[^23]. The evidence that existing groups sustain participation through relationship and meaning is as strong as evidence about human beings gets - congregations, twelve-step rooms, volunteer fire companies, co-ops, the Industrial Areas Foundation's seventy years of relational organizing[^24]. The evidence that such a group will adopt a tool over the one it has is plausible and not yet demonstrated, and it turns entirely on starting from the pain and not from the protocol.

Which groups first is a question with an honest answer. Groups organized around tangible local projects - the park, the school, the food, the flood - are the strongest entry: most precedents, most visible outcomes, lowest barrier, and the most natural path to a shared concern. Groups organized around the economic squeeze - buying clubs, tool libraries, mutual aid - are next; people show up for what saves them money. Faith communities carry more meaning than any other voluntary institution and must be entered through their own leaders, in their own language, or not at all. And the category I would put at the top for entry and the bottom for conversion is the purely social: the league, the dinner group, the meetup. Those groups have the most constant coordination pain and the weakest incumbents, and they are also the groups least likely to surface a shared concern or to want one. They widen the funnel. They convert last.

And conversion is where this part is weakest, and I will not hide it. A group that gets better at being itself does not thereby look outward; that is the oldest failure in civic life, the dinner club that stays a dinner club. My theory is that the conversion is not carried by the social layer alone. It is carried by what becomes possible once people can share their data selectively and privately and use applications that operate on it - once the room is one they can trust. The desire is not in question. Look at the digital nightclub: people plainly have opinions about decisions, and plainly want to express them, with an intensity that the feed monetizes and never resolves. The claim is that the same desire converts to participation in a secure network if the participants know it is trustworthy and private, and that is a claim about design, not about human nature, so it has to be engineered and measured the way platforms engineer and measure every conversion they care about, with experiments, not assumed. I have a counter-case in hand and I want you to see it. This year a community with every reason to trust its own forums learned that recordings, transcripts and notes from its calls had been stripped of context and published on X for malign purposes[^25]. That is what conversion on platforms looks like at its worst, and it is why people are right to hesitate. What the protocols change is that there is no platform holding the transcript to breach, and that disclosure is selective by construction. What they do not change is that a participant can still leak. Privacy by construction narrows the surface; it does not close it; and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a platform.

What this part needs: the ground. What it can do alone: make one group work better, which is a complete product. What breaks if it is removed: everything, because this is the participation problem, and no one has solved it from the governance end.

### Part two: small groups, deciding

Groups of three to six make sense of a situation, find its meaning, and reach a decision faster than larger groups and with more in the room than a pair or a person alone. That size is not a preference; it is where the evidence from Ostrom's commons and from sociocracy's circles and from Tang's rooms converges, and it is small enough that no one hides and large enough that no one dominates[^26]. When a concern exceeds the group's own competence, domain experts join it, and the way they join is designed against the obvious capture: the community nominates; a panel chosen by lot selects; and experts are paired adversarially, so that the room hears people who disagree, together, rather than one authority. Decisions are by consent - "safe enough to try," with objections recorded - or by rough consensus in the sense the Internet's own engineers gave the phrase: the authority is the body of people deciding, the outcome has sufficient support, it is documented and transparent, and every objection has been handled, which is not the same as every objector satisfied[^27]. Not consensus in the sense of a veto for anyone who holds out.

Here I should say what became of an idea I held earlier, because I expect you would have objected to it. I once imagined rewarding the small groups that decide well - a reputation score, perhaps a prediction market. I have dropped both. Any score becomes the thing people optimize, and a market in policy outcomes has been tried in the places that try such things, thinly, without result[^28]. What remains is a track record: what a group or an expert recommended, what was adopted, and what happened, carried as a credential issued by the fourth part of the system. Recognition, not reward. A record, not a score. That is also, if you noticed, the only kind of reputation the small town ever kept.

The failures this part is designed against: self-selection, which the breadth of the first part's groups is meant to answer; expert capture, which the lot and the pairing are meant to answer; and value conflict the process cannot hold, which no process fully answers, and which I will come back to. One failure I will name and not pretend to solve: there are places where the issue on the table sits on a wound that has not healed, where sense-making is not possible until something like reconciliation has happened, and a decision process is the wrong instrument. Public forums for truth and reconciliation exist for that, and the small room should know when to defer to one.

What this part needs: groups to deliberate, the fourth part to issue the record, the ground to hold it. What it can do alone: one group, one expert, one concern, recorded - which is valuable to that group. What breaks if it is removed: the third part aggregates raw opinion, which is a poll or a mob, and the epistemic leg of the bet disappears.

### Part three: the connective layer

This is the piece that is missing in the world, and the one this essay is actually proposing. It is the pathway from a group's rough consensus to a document that counts: a proposal with documented consent, authored by named real groups, aggregated across the groups that share the concern, carrying the weight of counted real people, delivered to the people with authority to act, and tracked afterward. Domain experts take the public's rough consensus of sentiment and values and shape it into recommendations and, where it comes to that, draft legislation. Every piece of this exists somewhere; none of it exists as standing infrastructure between communities and the people who govern them, and the absence is the whole reason that neighbors who care about each other do not, as a rule, become neighbors whose collective voice shapes anything[^29].

It is also where platforms have failed categorically, and where protocols have a chance, and I want to say why in plain terms, since this is my assessment rather than a finding. Every civic platform I have watched made its interface choices for its users rather than with them, because the owner and the developers held the pen; and then the data was breached, or could not be connected to anything else the community used, or the developers shipped a change that broke what people relied on; and each of those produced friction, and the friction produced distrust, and the distrust produced the empty platform that every civic-tech postmortem describes[^30]. Protocols invert the pen. The interface is a choice the community can make and remake; the data is relatively more secure because no one place holds all of it; and privacy is protected by how the thing is built rather than by a policy page.

One capability this layer needs does not exist yet, and I will describe it twice, once for each of you. For the many to build one document together - a proposal that a hundred groups have a hand in - you need a shared draft in which every change is recorded with who made it and why; in which anyone can propose a change without touching the original; in which a proposal is accepted or declined on the record; in which a disagreement can branch into two versions that sit side by side rather than one erasing the other; and in which every sentence links back to the conversation that produced it, with everyone who cares notified when it moves. Jordan, that is issues, discussions, forks, pull requests, blame and threaded links - git and the thing built on top of it, with the interface problem actually solved, which it never has been for anyone who is not a programmer. I concede it has not been built, even though git is among the most successful collaboration tools ever made in its own world; it is a major project and would take considerable resources. I will also say that I have talked with many developers who arrived at this design independently and want to build it, which is the kind of convergence that usually means something is ready[^31].

And now the hard part, which I will concede rather than solve. The failure of aggregation is mush: the flattening of real local differences into a consensus that nobody owns and nobody believes. Avoiding it needs processes that can hold polarities and the tension between them in a way that the people holding each pole find satisfying, or at least not insulting, and that is historically among the most difficult things human beings have attempted. It is the part of this system most in need of real innovation, and there are days I think it is impossible. The design rule that follows from that is the one Tang's experience supports: do not start there. The first issues a loop takes up are the urgent ones that no single department already owns and that do not split the room - the fraud ads, the flood plan, the vacant lot - and the loop earns the hard ones by having a record of the easy ones[^32]. Showing disagreement explicitly rather than averaging it, and leaving each group its own authorship, are the two devices I trust; they are what Polis got right. They are not a solution. They are a way to postpone the problem until the loop has standing enough to survive it.

What this part needs: the first two parts to supply it, the ground for counting and authorship, and the fourth section of this essay for anything to happen to its output. What it can do alone: carry one group's position to one council as something more than a public comment. What breaks if it is removed: good conversations with nowhere to go, which is the present.

### Part four: the feedback leg

A standing capacity to observe what happened after a decision - outcomes measured, compared where possible against similar places that decided differently, published - and feed what it finds back into the rooms and the documents, so that harm and the unintended get corrected rather than defended. It issues the track-record credentials the second part relies on. And its record is public, append-only and tamper-evident: what an official or a body committed to, on what date, and what happened, editable by no one, including us. The method is ordinary program evaluation, proven for half a century and almost never funded in civic work, which is itself a finding about where the missing leg went[^33].

This is the part that can start tomorrow, alone, before a single group has adopted a single tool. A public record of what a city council committed to and what came of it needs no new community, no deliberation, and no protocol beyond the log; it is valuable the day it exists; and it is the lowest-risk thing in this essay for anyone to fund. It is also what Porto Alegre lacked when it needed it most: a decade of measurable improvement that no one had recorded in a form the next administration could not wave away.

Its failures are the ones you would guess. No one pays for evaluation, which does not cascade immediately and does over years. The metrics become the targets, which is why the record holds facts about what happened rather than scores. The evaluator is captured, which is why the record is witnessed by more than one party and the evaluator's own record is in it.

### What this adds up to

Five parts: a ground that no one owns; groups that already exist, equipped; small rooms that decide; a layer that carries what they decide to where it counts; and a leg that watches what happens and says so. Each stands alone. Together they are the loop the existing system lacks - sense, decide, act, observe, correct - run at the speed the changes arrive, by the people the changes arrive on. What the loop does not yet have is any reason for the people who hold authority to listen to it, and that is the next section, where I confront the part of this argument that an earlier version of me got wrong.
